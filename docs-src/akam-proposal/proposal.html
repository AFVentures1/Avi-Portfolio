01 · Executive summaryThe loan is decided by what the file can't see

During my internship I studied how a microfinance bank decides whether to lend working capital to people who have no collateral, no credit score and no formal financial history.

I expected the missing data to be the main problem. What I found more interesting was what replaced it. Harvest timing, exchange-rate exposure and a borrower’s standing in her community often decided whether a loan could be repaid, yet none of these sits neatly in a conventional credit file.

This proposal sets out three small, AI-supported tools that could help loan officers record and use that information more consistently, together with the safeguards I think any such tool would need. They are ideas for discussion. None has been built, tested or adopted by AKAM or First MicroFinanceBank, and every lending decision would stay with trained staff.

02 · ContextWhere I worked

The Aga Khan Agency for Microfinance (AKAM) is part of the Aga Khan Development Network. It supports microfinance institutions that provide loans, savings and other financial services to families and small businesses that conventional banks tend to exclude.

My work focused on one of those institutions, First MicroFinanceBank Afghanistan (FMFB-A), which has operated since 2004 and whose shareholders are development institutions, including AKAM, the Aga Khan Foundation, KfW and IFC. I looked specifically at working-capital lending: short-term loans that let farmers, traders and small business owners buy the seeds, stock or materials they need before they are paid.

The internship was remote. Over video calls, FMFB-A staff walked us through the bank’s history, its results and live projects, and I spent time with HR, marketing and product colleagues learning how each team works. Between sessions I researched the challenges they described and prepared presentations exploring where AI might help.

03 · What I observedThree things the data doesn’t capture

A conventional lender relies on collateral, a credit score and financial statements. The borrowers I studied typically had none of them. The evidence that mattered came from elsewhere.

01Harvest timingSeasonal income revealed repayment capacity that monthly cash-flow analysis hid. A farmer can look unable to pay month by month yet repay easily after harvest. The problem was the schedule, not the borrower.
02Exchange ratesWhen a borrower’s inputs are imported, a weaker currency squeezes her margin without anything in her business changing. A loan that looks sound on paper can quietly become unaffordable.
03ReputationA borrower’s standing in her community carried economic value that no balance sheet records. References and a record of repaying informal loans worked as a kind of social collateral.

Is financial analysis about extracting the right answer from the data, or recognising what the data fails to capture?

A worked example (hypothetical figures)

Farida borrows 36,000 afghanis for seeds and supplies. Most of her income arrives in the weeks after harvest. The same borrower, with the same loan, gives two different answers depending on how the repayments are structured.

Equal monthly instalmentsLooks like a declineSeveral months show a shortfall, because repayments fall due before her crop is sold.
Harvest-aligned repaymentsLooks like an approvalPayments are scheduled around the months when her income actually arrives.

Farida is a hypothetical borrower used to illustrate the pattern. She is not a real FMFB-A client, and the figures are not bank data.

04 · Proposed ideasTools that help staff see more, not decide for them

Each idea is deliberately small. The aim is to help a loan officer record and weigh evidence that already exists, not to replace judgement with a score.

A

A seasonal cash-flow view

A simple assistant that helps an officer map a borrower’s income calendar, such as harvest, trading season or remittance months, and compare equal monthly instalments with a schedule aligned to when money actually arrives.

Why it matters: it turns “can she pay every month?” into the more useful “when can she pay?”

B

Structured notes for soft information

Loan officers already hear about references, group membership and informal repayment history. An AI-supported template could turn interview notes into a consistent, reviewable summary, so that evidence is recorded the same way across branches instead of being lost.

Why it matters: social collateral only helps a borrower if it is written down and weighed fairly.

C

A currency-exposure flag

A prompt that asks whether a borrower’s key inputs are imported and shows how her repayment capacity would change if the local currency weakened by, say, 10 to 30 percent.

Why it matters: it makes a hidden risk visible before the loan is agreed, when the structure can still change.

D

Support for internal teams

From my sessions with HR, marketing and product colleagues: AI-assisted first drafts of customer education material in local languages, reviewed by staff before use; a searchable guide to internal policies for new employees; and summaries of customer feedback that product teams can act on.

Why it matters: small time savings let staff spend more of their day with customers.

05 · SafeguardsWhat any tool like this would need

Microfinance exists to include people that other systems leave out. A careless tool could do the opposite, so these principles come before any feature.

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
People decideTools suggest and summarise. A trained officer makes and signs every lending decision, and can override any suggestion.
Fairness firstNo borrower should be penalised for lacking documents. Outputs would be checked regularly for differences between groups, including women and rural borrowers.
Privacy and consentCollect only what the decision needs, explain to borrowers how their information is used, and keep data within the institution’s own systems.
Explainable outputsEvery suggestion should show the reason behind it in plain language that an officer can repeat to a borrower.
Built for the contextWork in local languages, on low bandwidth and offline where needed, and fit the forms and routines staff already use.

06 · A cautious first stepHow the ideas could be tested

If any idea were worth exploring, I would suggest starting small and learning before building anything that affects a real decision.

Step 1ScopeAgree one idea, one branch and what a useful result would look like, with the teams involved.
Step 2PrototypeBuild a simple version using anonymised or past cases only.
Step 3Shadow testOfficers use it alongside their normal process, with no effect on decisions.
Step 4ReviewCompare notes with officers, check for bias, then decide whether to continue or stop.

07 · What I learnedThe loan didn’t change. Only its structure did.

The most useful lesson of the internship was that a lending decision depends as much on how a loan is structured as on who is borrowing. A repayment schedule, the currency a loan is written in and the way references are recorded can decide who carries the risk, and whether a capable borrower is approved at all.

The experience strengthened my interest in how financial institutions and technology can respond to practical needs, and led me towards a new interest in private equity, where assessing a business also means looking beyond reported numbers to understand how durable its cash flows really are.
